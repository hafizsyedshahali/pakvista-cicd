Lahore – City of Gardens

The cultural capital of Pakistan, where Mughal grandeur, Sikh legacy, colonial architecture, and modern vibrancy converge into one extraordinary city.

About Lahore

Lahore is Pakistan's second-largest city and the capital of Punjab province. With a history spanning more than a thousand years, it served as the seat of the Mughal Empire and later the Sikh Empire, leaving behind a remarkable concentration of monumental architecture that rivals any city in South Asia.

Beyond its historical treasures, Lahore is celebrated for its food — from the legendary nihari of the Walled City to the sizzling street food of Gawalmandi Food Street. It is also a centre of education, arts, and Urdu literature, earning it the title of Pakistan's cultural heart.

Badshahi Mosque

Built between 1671 and 1673 under the reign of Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb, the Badshahi Mosque stands directly opposite Lahore Fort across the historic Hazuri Bagh garden. Constructed from red sandstone and white marble inlay, it can accommodate up to 100,000 worshippers in its vast courtyard — one of the largest mosque courtyards in the world.

Its four towering minarets, each 53 metres tall, frame a central prayer hall crowned by three white marble domes. At the time of its completion, the Badshahi Mosque was the largest mosque in the world, a record it held for over 300 years. Visitors today can climb the minarets for a panoramic view of the Walled City, and the mosque museum houses sacred relics including strands of the Prophet Muhammad's hair.

Lahore Fort (Shahi Qila)

The Lahore Fort, known locally as Shahi Qila (Royal Citadel), is a UNESCO World Heritage Site that has been continuously occupied and modified by successive rulers since at least the 11th century. The present complex was substantially rebuilt and expanded by the Mughal emperors Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan, and Aurangzeb between the 16th and 17th centuries.

Among its 21 notable monuments, the Sheesh Mahal (Palace of Mirrors) is the most celebrated — its interior walls and ceilings are encrusted with thousands of tiny convex mirrors and coloured glass, designed to dazzle visitors with reflected candlelight. The Naulakha Pavilion, built entirely in white marble with semi-precious stone inlay, and the Diwan-e-Khas (Hall of Private Audience) are equally striking. The fort's Picture Wall — a 140-metre-long glazed tile mosaic — depicts elephants, polo matches, and mythological scenes, offering a vivid window into Mughal court life.

Shalimar Gardens

The Shalimar Gardens were commissioned by Mughal Emperor Shah Jahan in 1641 and completed in just one year by his governor Ali Mardan Khan. Laid out across three descending terraces — representing the three states of Faiz (benevolence), Faraj (pleasure), and Nuzhat (delight) — the gardens cover an area of approximately 16 hectares and were originally fed by a 161-kilometre canal from the River Ravi.

The gardens are famous for their 410 fountains, many of which still operate today, as well as their marble pavilions, ornamental pools, and fruit-bearing trees including mango, plum, and mulberry. The upper terrace was reserved exclusively for the emperor and his household, while the lower terraces were open to the public. Declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1981 alongside Lahore Fort, the Shalimar Gardens remain one of the finest surviving examples of Mughal garden design in the world.
